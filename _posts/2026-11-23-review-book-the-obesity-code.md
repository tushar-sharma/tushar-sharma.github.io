## Nov 23, 2026

What's a **set body weight**? First, what is **homeostasis**? The body tries to maintain a stable internal state. For example, normal human body temperature is around 98.6°F. If it changes, the body tries to bring it back toward its normal range. Similarly, one hypothesis says that the body defends a certain weight range. If our weight changes quickly, the body may adjust hunger and energy use to steer us back toward that range. Could this be one reason why maintaining weight loss is difficult?

There's a part of the human brain called the **hypothalamus**. What does it do? It helps regulate hunger and energy use. Can damage to it cause obesity? Some kinds of hypothalamic damage can disrupt weight regulation, but not every injury causes obesity. What does that teach us about how the brain influences body weight?

What's a hormone? It is a chemical messenger. It travels through the body and binds to receptors on certain cells, telling them to do something. For example, insulin signals muscle and fat cells to take up glucose from the blood.

Where does insulin come from? Beta cells in the pancreas make it. If the body produces too little insulin, or if its cells stop responding properly to insulin, blood glucose can remain too high. Is that what happens in diabetes?

What's leptin? It is a hormone made mainly by fat cells. It signals the brain about the body's stored energy and helps regulate hunger. If more body fat usually means more leptin, why can a person still feel hungry? Could the brain become less responsive to leptin?

What are the three types of macronutrients?

1. Fat

2. Carbohydrates

3. Protein

What happens after we eat them? Digestion breaks fats into fatty acids and glycerol, carbohydrates into simple sugars, and proteins into amino acids. But what about fiber? Fiber is a carbohydrate that our digestive enzymes cannot fully break down. Some of it passes through us, while gut microbes ferment some of it.

What happens to glucose after digestion? The body can use it immediately for energy. The liver and muscles can also store some of it as **glycogen**. When those needs are met, excess energy can eventually be stored as fat in subcutaneous tissue under the skin or as visceral fat around the organs.

What happens when the body needs energy later? The liver can break glycogen down and release glucose. Fat cells can release fatty acids and glycerol, which the body can also use for energy. So is glycogen itself the fuel that every cell consumes? Not exactly—glycogen is a stored form of glucose, while cells can use fuels such as glucose and fatty acids.
